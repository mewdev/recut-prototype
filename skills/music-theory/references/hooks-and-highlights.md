# Hooks and Highlights

Which existing section is the strongest keeper when a cut has to be short. This is selection/recognition, not songwriting — you're identifying which part of a recording already functions as its hook.

## What makes something a hook (detection criteria)

Score a candidate section/phrase against these; the more it has, the stronger a keeper it is:

- **Repetition** — does this melodic/lyrical idea recur multiple times in the song? A recurring idea is doing more structural work than a one-off.
- **Distinctiveness** — is it different from generic genre filler, or does it stand out (an unusual interval, a specific rhythm, a distinctive vocal tone/production sound)?
- **Brevity** — a true hook is short, 2–4 bars. If the "hook" candidate is a whole 16-bar section, the actual hook is probably a sub-phrase within it (check `phrases`).
- **Singability** — narrow range, mostly stepwise motion. Correlates with how memorable it reads to a general listener, not just a musician.
- **Early + recurring placement** — does it show up within roughly the first minute, and come back more than once? A "hook" buried at 2:30 that never repeats is not functioning as a hook.

## Don't default to "the chorus" without checking

The chorus is the most common hook location, but not the only one:

- **Post-chorus** (a short tag immediately after the chorus, often wordless — "oh-oh-oh" style): in a lot of modern pop/K-pop this is *the* most-remembered part, more than the chorus itself. If the source has a distinct, denser, recurring moment right after each chorus, check whether that — not the chorus proper — is the real hook.
- **Instrumental hook (riff)**: in rock/funk/EDM, an instrumental riff can be more iconic than any vocal section. If a distinctive instrumental figure recurs across the song (intro, between verses, outro), it's a legitimate keeper even without vocals.
- **Verse hook**: some songs have a recognizable recurring lyrical/melodic figure inside the verse itself, distinct from the chorus.

## Motif identity — recognizing a recurring hook across a recording

A motif's identity comes from pitch shape, rhythm, and harmonic context together — but rhythm is often the most recognizable component even when pitches vary slightly between occurrences (a hook restated a step higher in a final chorus is still "the same hook" to a listener). When checking whether a candidate section actually recurs elsewhere in the song (supporting its case as the hook), listen for rhythmic identity first, exact pitch match second.

## What this means for a short edit

- If forced to keep exactly one section, default to the strongest hook by the criteria above — usually the chorus, but verify against post-chorus/instrumental-hook candidates first.
- If keeping two sections, bias toward including the hook's *first* appearance (establishes it) plus its *strongest* appearance (often the final chorus, if the source elevates it — see `song-form-and-sections.md`), not two middle occurrences.
- A hook that only appears once in the source recording is a weaker keeper than one that recurs — cutting it out entirely loses less of the song's identity than cutting a 3-times-repeated hook down to zero appearances.

## Quick reference

| Situation | Bias toward |
|---|---|
| One section only, short teaser | Strongest-scoring hook by the criteria above (usually chorus, verify post-chorus) |
| Two sections | Hook's first appearance + its most elevated later appearance |
| No obvious vocal hook | Check for a recurring instrumental riff before concluding there's no hook |
| Multiple hook candidates score similarly | Prefer the one with the cleanest cadence at its boundaries (`cut-points-and-cadences.md`) — easier to cut cleanly |
